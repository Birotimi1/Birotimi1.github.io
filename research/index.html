Research

I use seismic waves to image Earth's shallow and deep structure, with a focus on sedimentary basins, crustal architecture, and their implications for seismic hazard. My work combines ambient noise seismology, Bayesian inversion methods, and multi-observable geophysical datasets to build high-resolution velocity models of the subsurface. I am also affiliated with the Syracuse University Center for Gravitational Wave Astronomy and Astrophysics where we are actively working on near-surface imaging for site selection for the next-generation gravitational-wave observatory Cosmic Explorer.

Hierarchical Bayesian Joint Inversion

My primary research develops a Hierarchical Bayesian Inversion framework for recovering 1D shear-wave velocity structure by jointly inverting multiple seismic observables: HVSR, Rayleigh wave ellipticity, phase velocity dispersion, and group velocity dispersion. The method uses Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling with hierarchical noise estimation, where each data type's uncertainty is treated as partially unknown and solved for during the inversion. This provides rigorous uncertainty quantification and reveals how much the data actually constrain the velocity model at each depth.

Sedimentary Basin Structure and Seismic Hazard

Sedimentary basins amplify and prolong ground shaking during earthquakes, making their geometry and velocity structure critical inputs for seismic hazard assessment. My research targets the Atlantic Coastal Plain, where Cretaceous-to-Quaternary sediments overlie crystalline basement across Georgia and South Carolina — a region home to millions of people and the site of the damaging 1886 Charleston earthquake. I map sediment thickness, basement depth, and the Moho across multiple deployment lines spanning the Coastal Plain and Charleston seismic zone, constraining how sediment properties vary spatially and influence site amplification.

Ambient Noise Seismology

I extract surface wave dispersion measurements and spectral ratios from continuous seismic records using ambient noise cross-correlation techniques. This approach enables high-resolution imaging in regions with low natural seismicity. My work spans the full processing pipeline — from raw waveform data through frequency-time analysis, ray-path tomography, and construction of dispersion curves and ellipticity measurements that feed into the joint inversion.

Crustal and Upper Mantle Imaging

The joint inversion framework recovers velocity structure from the near-surface through the crust and into the upper mantle. By combining short-period observables sensitive to shallow sediments with longer-period surface wave dispersion that senses deeper structure, the method resolves features across a wide depth range in a single consistent inversion. Key targets include the sediment-basement contact, mid-crustal velocity gradients, and the Moho transition, providing constraints on crustal thickness variations across the passive margin.

Geothermal Resource Characterization

I apply ambient noise methods to characterize subsurface structure in geothermal settings, where seismic velocity variations reflect temperature, fluid content, and rock properties relevant to resource assessment. This work involves processing dense array data to image shallow velocity heterogeneity and identify structural features associated with geothermal reservoirs.

Computational Methods and High-Performance Computing

My research relies on large-scale Bayesian computation, running hundreds of parallel sampling chains across broadband arrays. I develop modular inversion codes that incorporate simulated annealing, delayed rejection, hierarchical noise modeling, correlated error structures, and automated convergence diagnostics. I am also exploring physics-informed machine learning approaches for accelerating forward modeling and inverse problems in seismology.
